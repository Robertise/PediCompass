## 1. Introduction

Every year some 8 million children in developing countries die before they reach their fifth birthday; many during the first year of life. Eight in ten of these deaths are due to:
- Neonatal conditions
- Acute respiratory infections (mostly pneumonia)
- Diarrhoea (including dysentery)
- Malaria
- Severe malnutrition
- Or a combination of these conditions

The Pocket Book of Hospital Care for Children was first published in 2005 as a compilation of guidelines for managing these common childhood illnesses at the first-referral level in low-resource settings with limited equipment and staff capacity. Since then, new evidence has emerged and several WHO guidelines have been updated, requiring revision of the Pocket Book recommendations.

### 1.1 Target Audience

These recommendations are intended primarily for use by:
- Policy makers and child health programme managers
- Health-care providers at first-referral hospitals
- Professional bodies or technical partners involved in developing guidelines
- Medical training institutions in pre-service training

### 1.2 Objectives of the Recommendations

The main objectives are:
- Provide evidence-based recommendations for clinical management of common childhood illnesses
- Develop recommendations applicable in low-resource settings that maintain high standards of care
- Update the Pocket Book of Hospital Care for Children guidelines and IMCI clinical guidelines for management of common illnesses with limited resources

### 1.3 Financial Support

**Primary funding sources:**
- Better Medicines for Children Project (funded by Gates Foundation)
- WHO Department of Maternal, Newborn, Child and Adolescent Health
- WHO Collaborating Centre for International Child Health (CICH), Melbourne, Australia

### 1.4 Management of Conflict of Interest

All participants completed and signed declaration of interest forms. Decisions on verbal declarations were documented. Forms were reviewed and discussed by the WHO Guidelines Steering Committee before the meeting, with outcomes approved by the Director of the Department of Maternal, Newborn, Child and Adolescent Health.

**Participants with assessed conflicts deemed insufficient to exclude from participation:**

**Members excluded from final ratification:**
- Those with personal payments from commercial entities (e.g., National Institute of Health, advisory board positions)

### 1.5 Guiding Principles

The principles guiding these recommendations emphasize:

1. **Prioritize and optimize** the best options for treatment of common childhood illnesses to improve quality of care in hospitals in low-resource settings

2. **Promote best practices** based on high-quality evidence to deliver highest standards of care despite limited resources

3. **Analyze and balance** the benefits, risks, and feasibility of implementation in hospitals with limited resources

4. **Build consensus** among experts on recommendations in situations where there is insufficient evidence

## 2. Methodology and Process

### 2.1 Defining the Scope

The scope was defined by reviewing the 2005 edition of the Pocket Book and identifying recommendations requiring updates. This process included:

- **2009 consultative process:** Mapping the status of WHO guidelines and recommendations that contributed to the development of the Pocket Book

- **2010 Guidelines Steering Committee setup:** Established with members from key WHO departments:
  - Department of Maternal, Newborn, Child and Adolescent Health
  - Global Malaria Program (GMP)
  - Essential Health Technologies (EHT)
  - Mental Health and Substance Abuse (MSD)
  - Essential Medicines and Pharmaceutical Policies (EMP)
  - Stop TB (STB)
  - Nutrition for Health and Development (NHD)

- **Consultations with external experts:** Those implementing the Pocket Book or previously involved in writing/editing the first edition were consulted to identify gaps, emerging evidence, or changes to clinical practice

- **Field surveys feedback:** Two field surveys conducted in 2008 and 2009 on implementation and use of the Pocket Book provided input on priority areas for updates

**Categories of updates identified:**

1. **Revisions to align** with recently published Guideline Review Committee (GRC)-approved WHO guidelines
2. **Revisions of priority areas** that required collation, analysis, and synthesis of evidence to make recommendations

**Priority areas identified:**
- Management of selected neonatal conditions
- Management of cough or difficulty breathing
- Antibiotics for treatment of dysentery
- Management of conditions with fever
- Antibiotics for severe acute malnutrition
- Supportive care (oxygen therapy, choice of intravenous fluids, treatment of hypoglycaemia)

**Total PICO questions formulated:** 34 (see Annex 2)

### 2.2 Evidence Retrieval and Synthesis Process

**Timeline:** Throughout 2010, the Department of Maternal, Newborn, Child and Adolescent Health coordinated efforts to review and synthesize evidence.

**Literature search:** Conducted in July 2010 to identify high-quality systematic reviews

**Databases searched:**
- Cochrane Database
- OVID-Medline
- Medline/PubMed
- Embase
- CENTRAL
- NLM Gateway
- WHO regional databases

**Methodology:** Systematic reviews and meta-analyses followed the methodology recommended by the GRC as described in Version 5.1.0 of the Cochrane Handbook for Systematic Reviews of Interventions.

#### Evidence Quality Assessment

Quality assessment evaluated:
- **Study design** - type and number of studies
- **Limitations** - conduct and analysis issues in individual studies
- **Consistency** - results across available studies
- **Precision** - confidence intervals (narrow vs. wide)
- **Directness/Applicability** - external validity for populations of interest, interventions, and low-resource settings
- **Magnitude of effect**
- **Presence or absence of dose response gradient**
- **Direction of plausible biases**

#### Evidence Quality Levels

| Level | Definition |
|-------|-----------|
| **High** | Further research is very unlikely to change confidence in the estimate of effect |
| **Moderate** | Further research is likely to have an important impact on confidence in the effect |
| **Low** | Further research is very likely to have an important impact and is likely to change the estimate |
| **Very Low** | Any estimate of effect is very uncertain |

### 2.3 Consensus Building and External Peer Review

**Process:**
- Draft recommendations with supportive evidence (systematic reviews, risk-benefit analysis, GRADE tables) were circulated to selected expert external reviewers and Pocket Book users
- Electronic management through EZcollab site for feedback
- Results of peer review used to modify draft recommendations before GDG presentation

**Expert Panel Meeting:**
- Held at WHO headquarters in Geneva, Switzerland, February 14-17, 2011
- Panel weighed quality of evidence, risks and benefits, acceptability, and feasibility
- Emphasis placed on values and feasibility of implementation in low-resource settings
- Ensured recommendations align with international standards of care
- Most decisions based on evidence from RCTs or large observational cohort data
- Where insufficient evidence existed, expert consensus was used

**Final recommendations reached by consensus** without need for voting. Panel provided advice on strategies to support implementation through Pocket Book and other quality-of-care tools.

#### Strength of Recommendations Assessment

| Strength | Rationale |
|----------|-----------|
| **Strong** | The panel is confident that the desirable effects of adherence to the recommendation outweigh the undesirable effects |
| **Conditional/Weak** | The panel concludes that desirable effects probably outweigh undesirable effects. However: the recommendation is only applicable to a specific group/population/setting OR where new evidence may change the risk-benefit balance OR where benefits may not warrant the cost/resource requirements in all settings |
| **No recommendation** | Further research is required before any recommendation can be made |

**Factors influencing recommendation strength:**
- Quality of evidence
- Balance between risks and benefits
- Acceptability (values and preferences)
- Cost and feasibility of implementation in low-resource settings

### 2.4 Review and Update of Recommendations

- Recommendations will be regularly updated as more evidence is collated and analyzed continuously
- Major reviews and updates at least every 3 to 5 years
- Where new data requires update of recommendations, online interim updates will be produced
- These recommendations will form part of a technical series of evidence behind guidelines
- Next major update planned for 2014

## 4. Recommendations

### 4.1 Newborn Conditions

#### 4.1.1 Vitamin K Prophylaxis in Newborns

**Recommendations:**
a) All newborns should be given 1 mg of vitamin K intramuscularly (IM) after birth (after the first hour by which the infant should be in skin-to-skin contact with the mother and breastfeeding should be initiated).

b) Neonates requiring surgical procedures, those with birth trauma, preterm newborns, and those exposed in utero to maternal medication known to interfere with vitamin K are at especially high risk of bleeding and must be given vitamin K (1 mg IM).

**Strength:** Strong recommendation  
**Evidence Quality:** Moderate quality evidence

**Remarks:** The panel put large value on the expected benefits of vitamin K prophylaxis which clearly outweigh the harms. Implementation was noted as feasible and cost affordable in most settings.

##### 5.1.1 Evidence and Summary of Findings

**Incidence data:**
- Classical vitamin K deficiency bleeding: 10 to 1500 per 100,000 neonates (without prophylaxis)
- Late Vitamin K Dependent Bleeding (VKDB): 5 to 80 per 100,000 neonates
- Higher end of ranges in less-developed countries

**RCT Evidence:** Only two RCTs conducted in 1960s examined vitamin K supplementation (0.1 to 5 mg IM):

- **Post-circumcision bleeding:** 39% reduction (NNT 21, 95% CI 13 to 50)
- **Non-circumcision bleeding:** 43% reduction (NNT 80, 95% CI 42 to 714)
- **Moderate or severe bleeding:** 81% reduction (NNT 75, 95% CI 48 to 177)
- **Severe bleeding:** 97% reduction (NNT 140, 95% CI 90 to 500)
- **No difference** between 0.1 mg or 5 mg of vitamin K
- **Quality:** Moderate to low quality evidence (see GRADE table A7.1)

**Subclinical deficiency:** Vitamin K prophylaxis reduces subclinical deficiency in first week of life (measured by PIVKA - protein induced by vitamin K deficiency). No significant difference between oral or IM vitamin K on subclinical deficiency.

**Prothrombin time (PT):** Studies examining PT showed none with PT outside normal range in intervention and control groups. Two studies examining prothrombin complex deficiency: IM prophylaxis reduced deficiency by 72%, oral prophylaxis by 50%.

**Late VKDB Prevention:**
- From two observational studies: Vitamin K prophylaxis at birth reduces late VKDB incidence substantially
- IM route significantly better (~97% effectiveness) than oral route (65-83%)
- Although late VKDB is rare, about one-third of infants with this condition die or have severe handicaps
- NNT from observational studies: ~18,000 (with 6 per 100,000 baseline incidence)
- If incidence 30 per 100,000, NNT ~3000
- **Quality:** Low or very low quality evidence

##### 5.1.2 Benefits and Risks

**Benefits:**
- Vitamin K prophylaxis significantly reduces bleeding risk in neonates
- 20 neonates need prophylaxis to prevent one circumcision bleeding case
- ~80 neonates need prophylaxis to prevent one non-circumcision bleeding case
- One late VKDB case prevented per ~3000 infants treated

**Risks:**
- First report of potential association between vitamin K and childhood cancer appeared in 1990
- Subsequent research has not substantiated this concern

##### 5.1.3 Acceptability and Feasibility

- Disease relatively rare despite effective intervention
- Current practice in most developed countries: vitamin K prophylaxis to all newborns after birth
- **Cost:** US$ 0.195 per 1 mg injection (additional costs for syringe, needle, supply)
- Cost-effectiveness questionable in low-resource settings

#### 4.1.2 Prophylactic Antibiotics in Newborns at Risk of Infection

**Indication:** Neonates with risk factors:
- Membranes ruptured > 18 hours before delivery
- Mother had fever > 38°C before delivery or during labour
- Amniotic fluid foul smelling or purulent

**Recommendation:** Treat with prophylactic antibiotics ampicillin (IM or IV) and gentamicin for at least 2 days. After 2 days, reassess; continue only if signs of sepsis or positive blood culture.

**Strength:** Weak recommendation  
**Evidence Quality:** Very low quality evidence

**Rationale:** Panel made recommendation based on expert consensus. Very low quality evidence that intervention may prevent neonatal sepsis. No evidence available to evaluate harms, risk of increasing antibiotic resistance, injection safety, and hospitalization duration concerns.

##### 5.2.1 Evidence and Summary of Findings

**Cochrane Review:** Included only two studies.

**Outcomes:**
- No deaths reported in either intervention or control groups in either study
- One study found no cases of sepsis in either group
- **Quality:** Very low from these two single studies (see GRADE table A7.2)

**Specific findings:**
- None of trial participants died in neonatal period
- Wolf (1976) study: 4 cases neonatal sepsis, all in control (selective treatment) group
- Difference statistically non-significant (49 participants, RR = 0.12, 95% CI 0.01 to 2.04)

**Overall assessment:** Data insufficient to provide evidence of beneficial effect of immediate prophylactic antibiotic versus selective use on either neonatal mortality or sepsis incidence. Trials were small and underpowered to detect effects on outcomes of interest. Overall quality of evidence is very low.

##### 5.2.2 Benefits and Risks

**Benefits:**
- Intervention likely to prevent neonatal sepsis

**Risks:**
- No evidence available to evaluate harms

##### 5.2.3 Acceptability and Feasibility

- IM antibiotic administration feasible in small hospitals
- **Cost:** Ampicillin 250 mg: US$ 0.18/vial; Gentamicin 10mg/ml: US$ 0.07/ml
- Cost of implementation affordable in most low-resource settings

#### 4.1.3 Skin-to-Skin Contact in First Hour of Life

**Recommendation:** Newborns without complications should be kept in skin-to-skin contact (SSC) with mothers during first hour after birth to prevent hypothermia and promote breastfeeding.

**Strength:** Strong recommendation  
**Evidence Quality:** Low quality evidence

**Rationale:** Panel recommendation based largely on expert consensus. Large value placed on expected benefits on breastfeeding rates. Low quality evidence but cost of implementation low and feasible to implement in low-resource settings.

##### 5.3.1 Evidence and Summary of Findings

**Cochrane Review (2007):** Very low quality evidence. Studied population and intervention generally in line with intervention of interest, except SSC started after first hour in some studies (results should be interpreted with caution).

**Implementation variations:** In many studies, intervention not initiated immediately after birth; duration ranged from few minutes to several hours. Common practice being implemented per WHO guidelines. No notable harm; expected benefits outweighed risks.

**Key evidence (very low quality, see GRADE table A7.3):**

1. **Breastfeeding at 1-4 months:** 
   - 10 studies, 552 participants: significant positive effects
   - OR = 1.8 (95% CI 1.08 to 3.07)
   - None examined impact on neonatal mortality

2. **Exclusive breastfeeding at 4-6 months:**
   - 1 trial, 92 participants
   - OR = 5.67 (95% CI 2.27 to 14.16)
   - Positive and statistically significant

3. **Mean duration of breastfeeding:**
   - 7 trials, 324 participants
   - WMD = 42.55 days (95% CI -1.69 to 86.79)
   - Not statistically significant

4. **Body temperature in second hour of life:**
   - 3 studies, 168 participants
   - Temperature measured at 90-120 minutes after SSC
   - All started intervention immediately after birth
   - First two showed positive, statistically significant effect
   - Third showed no significant difference
   - Pooled WMD: 0.25 degrees centigrade (95% CI -0.15 to 0.65)

**Study settings:** Few from developing countries; majority from high-income countries. No adverse effects shown.

##### 5.3.2 Benefits and Risks

**Benefits:**
- SSC positive effect on breastfeeding 1-4 months post-birth
- Positive effect on exclusivity of breastfeeding 4-6 months after birth (low quality evidence for both)
- Exclusive breastfeeding associated with improved survival; SSC expected to reduce neonatal mortality

**Risks:**
- No evidence of harm with intervention

##### 5.3.3 Acceptability and Feasibility

- Low cost, should be feasible to implement
- Requires acceptance by mother, family, and community
- Cultural barriers might need to be overcome for implementation

#### 4.1.4 Management of Neonatal Jaundice

**Recommendations:**

a) Term and preterm neonates with hyperbilirubinaemia should be treated with phototherapy or exchange transfusion guided by cut-off levels:

| Age | Phototherapy (≥35 weeks) | Phototherapy (<35 weeks/risk factors) | Exchange Transfusion (≥35 weeks) | Exchange Transfusion (<35 weeks/risk factors) |
|-----|--------------------------|---------------------------------------|--------------------------------|---------------------------------------------|
| **Day 1** | Any visible jaundice | Any visible jaundice | 260 mmol/l (15 mg/dL) | 220 mmol/l (10 mg/dL) |
| **Day 2** | 260 mmol/l (15 mg/dL) | 170 mmol/l (10 mg/dL) | 425 mmol/l (25 mg/dL) | 260 mmol/l (15 mg/dL) |
| **Day ≥3** | 310 mmol/l (18 mg/dL) | 250 mmol/l (15 mg/dL) | 425 mmol/l (25 mg/dL) | 340 mmol/l (20 mg/dL) |

b) Clinicians should ensure all infants routinely monitored for jaundice development. Serum bilirubin measured in those at risk:
- All babies if jaundice appears on day 1
- Preterm babies (<35 weeks) if jaundice appears on day 2
- All babies if palms and soles are yellow at any age

c) Phototherapy should be stopped once serum bilirubin ≤50 mmol/l (3 mg/dl) or below phototherapy threshold.

**Strength:** Weak recommendation  
**Evidence Quality:** Very low quality evidence

**Remarks:** Suggested levels represent consensus by expert panel based on very limited evidence; approximations consistent with other international guidelines. Emphasis on preventive actions and risk evaluation where lower exchange transfusion cut-offs may prevent additional kernicterus cases. Recommendation should be accompanied by description on phototherapy administration in clinical guidelines.

##### 5.4.1 Evidence and Summary of Findings

**Association between bilirubin and neurodevelopmental outcomes:**

Systematic review by NICE (UK) plus four additional studies identified. Three observational studies evaluated association of high serum bilirubin (>340 μmol/l) with adverse sequelae:

- **Two studies in term babies:** One found no association between hyperbilirubinaemia and IQ, abnormal neurological exam, or sensorineural hearing loss
- **One study (term babies):** Reported severe jaundice requiring exchange transfusion and early jaundice onset as statistically significant risk factors for hearing loss
- **One study (birth weight <1000g):** Found weak association between high bilirubin levels and neurodevelopmental impairment, hearing impairment, and psychomotor impairment

**NICE GDG Conclusion:** Term neonates with serum bilirubin >340 μmol/litre at increased risk of kernicterus.

**Age-specific bilirubin cut-offs:**

Two randomized trials identified addressing this question:
- **One high-quality study:** Extremely low birth weight population (birth weight <1000g; <1% of total) showing no role for aggressive phototherapy
- **Other trial:** Little advantage in starting phototherapy early in babies >2500g

**Evidence interpretation:** Currently used conventional cut-offs need no further reduction. However, "conventional" cut-offs somewhat different from current Pocket Book version. No direct evidence for exchange transfusion cut-off. Consensus cut-offs from NICE GDG somewhat lower than current Pocket Book version.

##### 5.4.2 Benefits and Risks

**Benefits:**
- Lower phototherapy cut-offs may avoid need for exchange transfusion in majority of jaundiced infants
- Lower exchange transfusion cut-offs may prevent additional kernicterus cases (no evidence for best cut-off; requires consensus)

**Risks:**
- Aggressive phototherapy may not be beneficial
- May be associated with adverse effects in non-specialized settings

##### 5.4.3 Acceptability and Feasibility

- Exchange transfusion only possible in tertiary care settings

#### 4.1.5 Empirical Antibiotics for Suspected Neonatal Sepsis

**Recommendations:**

a) Neonates with signs of sepsis should be treated with ampicillin (or penicillin) and gentamicin as first-line antibiotic treatment for at least 10 days.

b) If neonate with sepsis at greater risk of staphylococcus infection (extensive skin pustules, abscess, or omphalitis in addition to sepsis signs), give cloxacillin and gentamicin instead of penicillin and gentamicin.

c) Where possible, obtain blood cultures before starting antibiotics. If infant not improving in 2-3 days, change antibiotic treatment or refer to neonatologist for further management.

**Strength:** Strong recommendation  
**Evidence Quality:** Low quality evidence (first-line); Expert opinion (modifications)

**Panel consideration:** High antimicrobial sensitivity to third-generation cephalosporins noted. Lack of sufficient evidence to choose between beta-lactams plus gentamicin and 3rd generation cephalosporins. In developing countries, streptococci continue highly sensitive to beta-lactams, though sensitivity to gentamicin of S. aureus (40-95%) and gram-negative enteric bacteria (32-100%) variable. Crucial to monitor changes in causative organism patterns and antimicrobial susceptibility profiles since effects depend on antibiotic susceptibility patterns.

##### 5.5.1 Evidence and Summary of Findings

**Source:** Two existing Cochrane reviews concluded insufficient evidence to choose between different antibiotic combinations for first-line treatment of early or late neonatal sepsis [Mtitima 2004; Gordon 2005].

**Classification:**
- **Early sepsis:** Presenting within 72 hours after birth
  - Most common organisms: Group B streptococcus, gram-negative enteric bacteria (esp. E. coli)
- **Late sepsis:** Presenting after 72 hours after birth
  - Most common organisms: Strep. pneumoniae, Staphylococcus aureus, E. coli, gram-negatives

**RCT Evidence:** Four RCTs of 3rd generation cephalosporin monotherapy versus penicillin and aminoglycoside combination:
- One conducted in South Africa, others in Europe
- Two trials used cefotaxime, two used ceftazidime monotherapy
- Three trials: ampicillin or benzyl penicillin with gentamicin as control
- One trial: penicillin with netilmicin as control
- **Overall quality:** Low (see GRADE table A7.4)

**Outcomes:**
- **Mortality and cure rates:** Similar between intervention and control groups in 3 of 4 studies
- **South African study:** Significantly higher cure rate
- **Overall:** No difference in mortality or cure rates between groups
- **One study finding:** Ceftazidime plus ampicillin combination had higher cure rates than ampicillin-gentamicin combination

##### 5.5.2 Benefits and Risks

**Benefits:**
- Available evidence shows no difference in cure rates and mortality between 3rd generation cephalosporin monotherapy and penicillin-aminoglycoside combination
- Effects may vary in different settings depending on antibiotic susceptibility patterns
- 3rd generation cephalosporin benefits: good cerebrospinal fluid penetration, convenient once/twice daily administration

**Risks:**
- Ceftriaxone risks: displacement of bilirubin from albumin leading to higher free bilirubin and kernicterus risk, promotion of beta-lactamase resistance

##### 5.5.3 Acceptability and Feasibility

- Neonates with suspected sepsis require admission regardless of antibiotic choice
- Differences in administration convenience less important
- **Cost:** Ampicillin 250mg (US$ 0.18/vial), Gentamicin 10mg/ml (US$ 0.07/ml), Ceftriaxone 250mg (US$ 0.44/vial)
- Cefotaxime more expensive than ceftriaxone

#### 4.1.6 Head or Whole Body Cooling in Hypoxic Ischaemic Encephalopathy

**Recommendation:** Head or whole body cooling should NOT be done outside well-resourced, tertiary neonatal intensive care units, because there is potential for harm from this therapy in low-resource settings.

**Strength:** Strong recommendation  
**Evidence Quality:** Moderate quality evidence

**Rationale:** Panel recommendation based on potential harm from body cooling if applied outside NICU where mechanical ventilation, sedation, and supportive care cannot safely be provided. In low-resource settings, no evidence supporting this therapy; expensive. In well-resourced tertiary NICUs, cooling improves survival and reduces disability for term neonates with asphyxia and hypoxic ischaemic encephalopathy.

##### 5.6.1 Evidence and Summary of Findings

**Mechanism:** Damage from lack of oxygen continues for some time after birth. Induced hypothermia (cooling) helps stop further damage by reducing brain cell damage.

**RCT Evidence:** Eight RCTs included, comprising 638 term infants with moderate or severe encephalopathy and evidence of intrapartum asphyxia [Jacobs 2007]:
- Six pilot studies: three in single centres (New Zealand, Turkey, China); others multi-centre (Australia, North America)
- All 8 trials: term newborns with moderate or severe encephalopathy and intrapartum hypoxia-ischaemia evidence, no obvious congenital abnormalities
- All: randomization with intervention initiated by 6 hours of age (mean 1.9 to 4.6 hours)

**Key Findings:**

1. **Mortality or major neurodevelopmental disability to 18 months:**
   - Typical RR: 0.76 (95% CI 0.65-0.89)
   - Typical RD: -0.15 (95% CI -0.24 to -0.07)
   - NNT: 7 (95% CI 4-14)
   - Statistically significant and clinically important

2. **Mortality alone:**
   - Typical RR: 0.74 (95% CI 0.58-0.94)
   - Typical RD: -0.09 (95% CI -0.16 to -0.02)
   - NNT: 11 (95% CI 6-50)
   - Statistically significant

3. **Neurodevelopmental disability in survivors:**
   - Typical RR: 0.68 (95% CI 0.51-0.92)
   - Typical RD: -0.13 (95% CI -0.23 to -0.03)
   - Statistically significant

4. **Overall statistics:**
   - Relative risk reduction: 24%
   - Absolute risk reduction: 15%
   - NNT: 7

5. **Adverse effects:**
   - Increase in inotrope support need (borderline significance)
   - Significant increase in thrombocytopaenia

##### 5.6.2 Benefits and Risks

**Benefits:**
- Clear benefits of cooling on survival and neurodevelopment outweigh short-term adverse effects

**Risks:**
- Possible increased risk of thrombocytopaenia and hypotension from therapeutic hypothermia

##### 5.6.3 Acceptability and Feasibility

- Parents likely willing to allow procedure as reasonably expect cooling would decrease baby's chance of dying and may lessen major disability occurrence if survival

#### 4.1.7 Antibiotics for Treatment of Necrotizing Enterocolitis

**Recommendation:** Young neonates with suspected necrotizing enterocolitis (NEC) should be treated with IV or IM ampicillin (or penicillin) and gentamicin as first-line antibiotic treatment for 10 days.

**Strength:** Strong recommendation  
**Evidence Quality:** Low quality evidence

**Remarks:**
- Common practice to add metronidazole to NEC treatment (no evidence for benefit, but covers GI anaerobic bacteria like Bacteroides fragilis)
- Vancomycin and ceftriaxone combination suitable alternative (evidence only from tertiary western ICUs)
- Vancomycin and 3rd generation cephalosporins expensive; not feasible as first-line for NEC in low-resource settings

##### 5.7.1 Evidence and Summary of Findings

**Disease characteristics:** NEC serious disease with varying mucosal necrosis degrees; predominantly premature infants. Underlying pathogenesis unclear; pathogenic organisms not isolated in many cases. Clustering and outbreaks suggest infective component.

**Study identification:** Four studies directly comparing antibiotic regimens in NEC.
- **One non-concurrent cohort study (Belgium):** Excluded - effect of metronidazole addition to existing regimen couldn't be separated from other simultaneous modifications (different surgical approaches, anaesthesia changes, ICU management modifications)

**Key Studies:**

1. **Scheifele 1987:**
   - 46 NEC cases (1982-83): ampicillin and gentamicin
   - 44 NEC cases (1984-85): vancomycin and ceftriaxone
   - Birth weight >2200g: no statistical differences in outcomes; infrequent complications in both groups
   - Birth weight <2200g: Vancomycin group showed statistically lower rates:
     - Death (p=0.048)
     - Surgery need (p=0.04)
     - Culture positive peritonitis (p=0.01)
     - Major complications (p=0.004): peritonitis, strictures, feed intolerance, recurrent NEC, thrombocytopenia
   - **Quality:** Very low evidence

2. **Faix 1998 (RCT):**
   - 42 premature infants with radiographically confirmed NEC
   - Ampicillin and gentamicin vs. ampicillin, gentamicin, and clindamycin
   - **Finding:** Increased complicating strictures in clindamycin group (NNT harm 3, 95% CI 2-13)
   - Longer time to restart feeds: 8.4 days vs. 22.3 days
   - No significant differences in death or gangrene/perforation rates
   - **Study termination:** Early due to significantly higher stricture rates in clindamycin group
   - **Quality:** Low evidence (see GRADE table A7.5)

3. **Hansen 1987 (RCT):**
   - 20 infants with NEC
   - IV ampicillin and gentamicin vs. IV ampicillin and gentamicin with oral gentamicin for 4 days
   - Enrolled infants more mature: 34.7±1.3 vs. 35.6±1.1 weeks gestation; higher birth weight
   - **Findings:** No significant differences (p>0.05) in outcomes:
     - Days with peritonitis
     - Days with pneumatosis
     - Major complications (perforation, stricture, ileus, death)
   - **Limitations:** Underpowered to detect most differences; blinding and allocation concealment poorly defined
   - **Quality:** Low evidence

##### 5.7.2 Benefits and Risks

**Benefits:**
- Vancomycin and ceftriaxone may be better than ampicillin and gentamicin (low confidence - factors other than antibiotics may have contributed in non-concurrent study)
- No evidence to judge metronidazole benefits

**Risks:**
- Clindamycin resulted in significantly higher stricture formation rate
- No evidence to judge metronidazole risks

##### 5.7.3 Acceptability and Feasibility

- Vancomycin and ceftriaxone combination not feasible, expensive as first-line NEC antibiotic treatment

#### 4.1.8 Kangaroo Mother Care

**Recommendation:** Low birth weight (LBW) neonates weighing <2000g who are clinically stable should be provided Kangaroo Mother Care (KMC) early in first week of life.

**Strength:** Strong recommendation  
**Evidence Quality:** Moderate quality evidence

**Remarks:** Although no evidence for KMC in babies >2000g, may be beneficial in neonates with temperature instability. Implementation requires description on KMC provision.

##### 5.8.1 Evidence and Summary of Recommendations

**Definition of KMC:** Combination of continuous skin-to-skin contact, support for exclusive breastfeeding or appropriate feeding, and early recognition and response to complications.

**Systematic Review:** One recent review [Lawn 2010] aimed to assess effect on neonatal mortality and morbidity from preterm birth complications of KMC versus no care or conventional care. Preterm death defined as neonatal death in baby with birth weight <2000g. Studies with KMC commencement after first week of life were excluded.

**Included Studies:** 15 studies total:
- 9 randomized controlled trials (RCTs)
- 6 observational studies
- All from low or middle-income countries

**Mortality Data:**

1. **RCT Analysis:** 6 RCTs reported mortality data; 3 included in analysis
   - **Two studies excluded:** Median KMC initiation >1 week after birth
   - **Bangladesh community trial:** Excluded - important methodological limitations, not in health facilities
   - **Three remaining trials** (India, Colombia, Ethiopia):
     - **RR 0.49** (95% CI 0.29-0.82)
     - 988 infants
     - **Quality:** High quality evidence (see GRADE table A7.6)

2. **Observational Studies:** Three observational studies (8,257 infants)
   - **RR 0.68** (95% CI 0.58-0.79) - mortality reduction
   - Heavily influenced by South African study (96% of pooled estimate weight)

**Serious Morbidity Data:**

Five RCTs reported serious morbidity data (severe pneumonia, sepsis, jaundice, other severe illness):
- Variations in study definitions and heterogeneity in results (I² = 70%, P <0.01)
- **KMC associated with morbidity reduction:** RR 0.34 (95% CI 0.17-0.65)
- 1,520 babies involved
- **Quality:** Moderate quality evidence (see GRADE table A7.6)

##### 5.8.2 Benefits and Risks

**Benefits:**
- **High quality evidence:** KMC leads to significant neonatal mortality reduction for babies <2000g in low- to middle-income countries
- **Moderate quality evidence:** Reduces serious neonatal morbidity compared to conventional care
- Additional benefits: improved breastfeeding and bonding

**Risks:**
- Possible risks: apnoea and bradycardia in some babies
- KMC should start once LBW infants clinically stable

##### 5.8.3 Acceptability and Feasibility

- Benefits highly valued by families, health providers, programme managers/policy makers
- Additional training and counselling costs for KMC offset by conventional care costs (incubator use)

#### 4.1.9 Prevention of Hypothermia After Birth in LBW Infants

**Recommendation:** Low birth weight (LBW) neonates weighing >1200g without complications and clinically stable should be put in skin-to-skin contact with mother soon after birth and after drying thoroughly to prevent neonatal hypothermia.

**Strength:** Weak recommendation  
**Evidence Quality:** Low quality evidence

**Remarks:** Panel noted plastic wraps beneficial in reducing hypothermia. Possible risks of using plastic wraps in developing country settings not known. Very little developing country experience; further research needed on plastic type, practicality, and appropriateness in low-resource settings.

##### 5.9.1 Evidence and Summary of Findings

**Systematic Review:** One recent Cochrane review [McCall 2010] assessed interventions to prevent hypothermia in preterm/LBW infants.

**Three interventions evaluated:**
1. Plastic wraps
2. Plastic caps
3. Skin-to-skin contact

**Study criteria:** Initiated within 10 minutes of birth. Hypothermia defined as core body temperature <36.5°C on NICU admission or up to 2 hours after birth.

**Evidence quality:** Low to moderate for caps or plastic wraps; moderate for skin-to-skin contact reducing hypothermia in LBW newborns (GRADE table A7.7)

**Plastic Wraps:**
- **<28 weeks gestation:** Effective in reducing heat loss
  - 4 studies, n=223
  - WMD 0.68°C (95% CI 0.45-0.91)
- **28-31 weeks gestation:** Not effective (1 study, n=41)
- **<29 weeks gestation:** Significantly reduced hypothermia risk
  - 2 studies, n=152
  - RR 0.66 (95% CI 0.51-0.84)
  - RD -0.27 (95% CI -0.41 to -0.13)
  - NNT 4 (95% CI 2-8)

**Plastic Caps:**
- **<29 weeks gestation:** Effective in reducing heat loss
  - 1 study, n=64
  - MD 0.80°C (95% CI 0.41-1.19)
- **Hypothermia risk reduction:**
  - RR 0.48 (95% CI 0.32-0.73)
  - RD -0.47 (95% CI -0.67 to -0.27)
  - NNT 2 (95% CI 2-4)

**Skin-to-Skin Contact (1200-2199g birth weight):**
- Significantly reduced hypothermia risk within 6 hours of birth vs. conventional incubator care
- 1 study, n=31
- RR 0.09 (95% CI 0.01-0.64)
- RD -0.56 (95% CI -0.84 to -0.27)
- NNT 2 (95% CI 1-4)

##### 5.9.2 Benefits and Risks

**Benefits:**
- Plastic wrap or cap immediately after birth prevents hypothermia in <29 weeks gestation infants
- For LBW >1200g, skin-to-skin contact immediately after birth effectively prevents hypothermia

**Risks:**
- No reported harms evidence available

##### 5.9.3 Acceptability and Feasibility

- Plastic wraps relatively low cost, easy to use, acceptable to LBW newborn parents

### 4.2 Treatment of Cough and Difficulty in Breathing (Pneumonia)

#### 4.2.1 Non-Severe Pneumonia with Wheeze

**Recommendation:** Antibiotics are NOT routinely recommended for children with non-severe pneumonia (fast breathing, no chest indrawing or danger signs) with wheeze but no fever (temperature <38°C), as cause most likely viral.

**Strength:** Strong recommendation  
**Evidence Quality:** Low quality evidence

**Implementation requirement:** Applicable only where health workers can assess "wheeze". Health workers must be trained in auscultation and caregiver counseling.

##### 6.1.1 Evidence and Summary of Findings

**Key Observations:**
- WHO criteria for pneumonia perform poorly in children with wheeze
- Addition of fever to WHO criteria improves diagnostic accuracy in this group
- Children with wheeze and no fever very unlikely to have bacterial pneumonia
- In Asia, up to 60% of children with non-severe pneumonia reported to have audible wheeze by auscultation

**RCT Evidence:** Two recent RCTs provide direct evidence:

1. **Hazir et al (2010) - Pakistan:**
   - **Design:** Double-blinded, randomized, placebo-controlled trial
   - **Intervention:** Oral amoxicillin vs. placebo
   - **Settings:** 4 centres in Pakistan
   - **Population:** 900 children randomized (450 per group)
   - **Baseline characteristics:**
     - 73-80% history of fever
     - 30-50% temperature >37.8°C
     - 50-60% wheeze on examination; 5-6% wheeze history
   
   - **ITT Analysis Results:**
     - Day 3: 50 amoxicillin (11.1%) vs. 45 placebo (10%) failed
     - Difference not statistically significant: OR 1.13 (95% CI 0.72-1.76)
     - Day 5 cumulative failure: Similar (OR 0.87, 95% CI 0.61-1.24)
     - Per-protocol: 7.2% amoxicillin vs. 8.3% placebo failed day 3 (OR 0.85, 95% CI 0.50-1.43)
     - No difference in relapse by day 14 (OR 0.58, 95% CI 0.11-2.81)
   
   - **Treatment failure predictors:**
     - History of difficulty breathing (OR 2.86, 95% CI 1.13-7.23)
     - Temperature >37.8°C (OR 1.99, 95% CI 1.37-2.90)
   
   - **Secondary outcomes:** No differences between groups in:
     - Number requiring antibiotic change/initiation
     - Number developing danger signs
     - Hospitalization requirement
     - Deaths (none reported)

2. **Awasthi et al (2008) - India:**
   - **Design:** Double-blinded, randomized, multi-centre non-inferiority trial
   - **Population:** 1,671 children (836 placebo, 835 amoxicillin)
   - **Inclusion:** WHO non-severe pneumonia, audible/auscultatory wheeze, normal chest X-ray, no response to 3 inhaled salbutamol doses
   - **Treatment:** Outpatient oral salbutamol and either amoxicillin or placebo
   - **Primary outcome:** Treatment failure at day 4 (WHO severe/very severe pneumonia, hypoxaemia SpO₂<90%, persistent non-severe pneumonia/wheeze/fever)
   
   - **Baseline characteristics:** Similar between groups; adherence >95%; RSV in 6.2% placebo vs. 5.1% amoxicillin
   - **Clinical wheeze:** ~15% audible; rest detected on auscultation
   
   - **ITT Results:**
     - Treatment failure: 24% placebo vs. 19.9% amoxicillin (difference 4.2%, 95% CI 0.2-8.2)
     - Placebo inferior to amoxicillin
     - **Clinical failure from severe/very severe pneumonia or hypoxaemia:** Similar between groups
     - Relapse rates: No difference

**Observational Studies Evidence:**

1. **Hazir et al (2004):**
   - 62% of 1,004 non-severe pneumonia with wheeze children responded to bronchodilators
   - Among responders: only 15% clinical deterioration at follow-up
   - **Deterioration predictors:**
     - Fever >38°C (OR 6.61, 95% CI 2.73-16.11)
     - Fever history (OR 2.11, 95% CI 1.44-3.03)
     - Age 1-11 months (OR 1.83, 95% CI 1.26-2.66)

2. **Lochindarat 2008:**
   - Prospective observational study of fast breathing/lower chest indrawing and wheeze children
   - 263 non-severe pneumonia with wheeze responsive to bronchodilator children
   - **Outcomes:**
     - 85% responded to bronchodilators; discharged on oral salbutamol, no antibiotics
     - Symptoms resolved: 96% by day 3, 97% by days 5-7
     - Only independent deterioration predictor: age 1-11 months
   - **Non-response predictors to bronchodilators:**
     - Age 12-59 months
     - Fever history
     - No family wheeze history
     - Fever >38.0°C
     - Lower chest indrawing presence

3. **Kabir et al (2009) - Bangladesh:**
   - Non-blinded, randomized multi-centre bronchiolitis trial
   - <24 months, runny nose, cough, breathing difficulty, chest indrawing, rhonchi
   - **Groups:** Ampicillin 7 days; oral erythromycin 7 days; no antibiotics
   - All received: nebulized salbutamol, oxygen (if SpO₂<90%), IV fluids, NG feeding, oropharyngeal suction
   - **Results:**
     - 327 children; 10% drop-out
     - No significant clinical improvement differences between groups
     - No-antibiotic group: significantly shorter hospitalization (p<0.001)
     - Breathing/chest symptoms gradually improved after day 4

4. **Cardoso et al (2010) - Brazil:**
   - Children 2-59 months with acute lower respiratory tract infections
   - 5 hospital paediatric emergency departments
   - Prospective recruitment; diagnoses by three physicians (blinded to each other): admitting paediatrician, chest physician, radiologist
   - **Data analyzed:** 390 children; 153 had chest X-rays
   - **Diagnoses:** 28 acute bronchitis (7%), 7 bronchiolitis (2%), 117 wheezing (30%), 168 recurrent wheezing (43%), 70 pneumonia (18%; 15 also recurrent wheezing)
   - **Agreement:** High between paediatricians and chest physicians (κ=0.86, 95% CI 0.82-0.90)
   
   - **WHO Criteria Performance Overall:**
     - Sensitivity: 84%, Specificity: 19%
     - With fever: Sensitivity 81%, Specificity 46%
   
   - **WHO Criteria in Wheeze Children:**
     - Sensitivity: 90%, Specificity: 12%
     - With fever: Sensitivity 85%, Specificity 42%
   
   - **Without Wheeze:**
     - Sensitivity: 76%, Specificity: 62%
     - With fever: No effect; Specificity improved to 70%

5. **Mathews et al 2009 - USA:**
   - Prospective cohort, paediatric emergency department
   - Children <18 years with wheeze on exam; underwent chest X-ray pneumonia evaluation
   - **Population:** 526 patients; 75% <5 years old
   - **Pneumonia:** 26 patients (radiographically defined; 2 blinded radiologist consensus)
   
   - **Fever findings:**
     - History of fever: 81% pneumonia vs. 58% no pneumonia (Positive LR 1.39, 95% CI 1.13-1.70; p<0.02)
     - Triage temperature <38.0°C: 50% pneumonia vs. 25% no pneumonia (Positive LR 2.03, 95% CI 1.34-3.07; p<0.01)
     - ED temperature <38.0°C: 73% pneumonia vs. 38% no pneumonia (Positive LR 1.92, 95% CI 1.48-2.49; p<0.001)
     - **Afebrile (temperature <38°C) with wheezing:** Pneumonia very uncommon (2.2%, 95% CI 1.0-4.7)

##### 6.1.2 Benefits and Risks

**Benefits:**
- Rationalizes antibiotic therapy
- Avoids patient side effects
- Reduces antimicrobial resistance risk

**Risks:**
- Withholding antibiotics from children with bacterial pneumonia

##### 6.1.3 Acceptability and Feasibility

- Withholding antibiotics may be less acceptable to caregivers
- Requires health worker training for caregiver education and counseling
- Close follow-up necessary
- Health workers require training in auscultation

#### 4.2.2 Non-Severe Pneumonia Without Wheeze

**Recommendations:**

a) Children with non-severe pneumonia (fast breathing, no chest indrawing or danger signs) should be treated with oral amoxicillin.

**Exception - HIV patients:**
- **Low HIV prevalence:** Amoxicillin ≥40 mg/kg/dose twice daily for 3 days
- **High HIV prevalence:** Amoxicillin ≥40 mg/kg/dose twice daily for 5 days

b) Children with non-severe pneumonia failing first-line amoxicillin treatment should have option of referral to facility with appropriate second-line treatment.

**Strength:** Weak recommendation  
**Evidence Quality:** Moderate quality evidence

**Rationale:** Recommendations based on mostly Asian RCTs, more likely low HIV prevalence settings. Amoxicillin recommended based on values and preferences; dispersible amoxicillin becoming more available and cost decreasing. Moderate quality evidence relates to amoxicillin duration comparison (3 vs. 5 days) showing no difference in cure and clinical failure rates.

##### 6.2.1 Evidence and Summary of Findings

**Key Finding:** Randomized trials have not shown significant effectiveness differences of different oral antibiotics for non-severe pneumonia treatment.

**Systematic Review:** [Sajwani 2010] identified trials comparing oral antibiotics and short-course (3 days) versus long-course (5 days) therapy.

**Antibiotic Choice:**

1. **Oral co-trimoxazole vs. Amoxicillin (3 studies, 3,952 children):**
   - 2,067 co-trimoxazole; 1,885 amoxicillin
   - 2 months - 59 months age with WHO non-severe pneumonia
   - **Study types:** 2 double-blinded RCTs with adequate allocation concealment; 1 open-label, cluster-randomized
   - **Outcomes:**
     - Clinical failure: RR 1.09 (95% CI 0.93-1.27; 3 studies) - no difference
     - Cure rate: RR 0.99 (95% CI 0.96-1.01; 2 studies) - no difference
   - [Strauss 1998]: Included severe pneumonia subset; higher failure rates for co-trimoxazole vs. amoxicillin in severe pneumonia

2. **Oral Levofloxacin vs. Co-amoxiclavulanic acid:**
   - 1 open-label RCT, 708 children (270 <5 years)
   - Chest X-ray infiltrates and ≥2 pneumonia clinical indicators
   - **Results:** No difference in cure rate at 10-17 days post-treatment (<5 years: RR 1.02, 95% CI 0.93-1.11)

3. **Azithromycin vs. Co-amoxiclavulanic acid:**
   - 2 RCTs, 393 children with chest X-ray and pneumonia clinical findings
   - **Results:** Treatment failure at 2 weeks not significantly different (RR 1.20, 95% CI 0.45-3.21)

4. **Azithromycin vs. Amoxicillin (Chile study):**
   - 47 children (1 month - 14 years) with X-ray and classical bacterial pneumonia signs
   - **Results:** 
     - Chest X-ray normalized day 7: azithromycin significantly more often (p<0.009)
     - Clinical response days 3, 7, 14: Not significantly different

5. **Amoxicillin vs. Co-amoxyclavulanic acid (Nigeria):**
   - Open-label RCT, 100 children (2 months - 12 years) with non-severe pneumonia
   - **Results:** Co-amoxyclavulanic acid superior cure rates (OR 10.44, 95% CI 2.85-38.21)
   - **Limitation:** Inadequate allocation concealment

6. **Azithromycin vs. Erythromycin:**
   - Open randomized multi-centre study, 68 children (2-16 years) radiologically proven pneumonia
   - **Results:** No significant clinical success or adverse outcome differences

**Aetiology and Antibiotic Susceptibility [Grant et al 2009]:**

Common bacterial pathogens from severe pneumonia inpatient studies:
- Streptococcus pneumoniae: 17-37%
- Haemophilus influenzae: 0-31%
- Staphylococcus aureus: 1-33%
- ~25% community-acquired pneumonia have positive viral diagnostic test

**Limited data on non-severe pneumonia aetiology**; antibiotics targeted at severe pneumonia pathogens. Limited data on antibiotic resistance effect on pneumonia. Co-trimoxazole in-vitro resistance correlates with acute otitis media poor clinical outcome. [Strauss et al] showed higher failure rates for children with severe pneumonia receiving co-trimoxazole vs. amoxicillin (unclear if resistance-related).

**Treatment Duration:**

Four studies with 6,513 participants compared same antibiotic for different durations (3 vs. 5 days):
- 2 studies: amoxicillin three times daily (30-50 mg/kg/day)
- 2 studies: co-trimoxazole
- All randomized, double-blinded, placebo-controlled RCTs
- **Pooled analysis:**
  - Cure rates: RR 0.99 (95% CI 0.98-1.01, n=5,892) - no difference
  - Failure rates: RR 1.07 (95% CI 0.92-1.25, n=5,892) - no difference
  - Relapse rates: RR 1.06 (95% CI 0.82-1.37, n=2,753) - no difference

##### 6.2.2 Benefits and Risks

**Benefits:**
- Amoxicillin and co-trimoxazole most widely available oral antibiotics
- [Strauss et al] suggests higher treatment failure in severe pneumonia with co-trimoxazole vs. amoxicillin
- **Short-term therapy (3 days) benefits vs. long-term (5 days):**
  - Increased compliance
  - Lower cost
  - Lower potential antibiotic side effect exposure
  - Low HIV prevalence trials show equal effectiveness
- **Caution:** Shortened courses not as well-studied in high HIV prevalence settings where substantially greater severe bacterial pneumonia burden exists in HIV-infected persons

##### 6.2.3 Acceptability and Feasibility

- Amoxicillin and co-trimoxazole widely available
- Oral amoxicillin preferred first-line over co-trimoxazole as effective against both non-severe and severe pneumonia in low HIV settings
- High HIV settings: oral amoxicillin preferred as co-trimoxazole recommended for PCP prophylaxis
- Oral amoxicillin also recommended first-line for children with severe pneumonia tolerating feeds; simplifies uptake
- **Cost:** Amoxicillin 250mg (US$ 0.018/capsule, US$ 0.022/dispersable tablet); standard co-trimoxazole (US$ 0.013) [UNICEF 2010]

#### 4.2.3 Severe Pneumonia

**Recommendations:**

a) Children aged 2-59 months with severe pneumonia (chest indrawing) should be treated with oral amoxicillin ≥40 mg/kg/dose twice daily for 5 days.

**Strength:** Strong recommendation  
**Evidence Quality:** Moderate quality evidence

**Applicability note:** May be applicable to community or outpatient level case management; implementation should be guided by clinical context and setting. NOT applicable in HIV/AIDS-infected children (follow current HIV-specific guidelines).

b) In children infected with HIV/AIDS, specific guidelines for severe pneumonia treatment in HIV context should be followed.

**Strength:** Strong recommendation  
**Evidence Quality:** Low quality evidence

##### 6.3.1 Evidence and Summary of Findings

**Systematic Review:** [Jabeen et al 2010] identified five studies comparing oral with injectable antibiotics for WHO-defined severe pneumonia.

**Overall Assessment:** Moderate quality evidence that oral amoxicillin is effective and safe alternative to parenteral antibiotics in low HIV settings (see GRADE tables A7.10 & A7.11). Oral amoxicillin effective and safe alternative for severe pneumonia children (chest indrawing) without other serious signs or symptoms.

**Key RCT Studies:**

1. **Campbell et al (1988):**
   - **Design:** Oral co-trimoxazole vs. IM procaine penicillin followed by oral ampicillin
   - **Population:** 134 children with non-severe or severe pneumonia
   - **Results (Day 7 follow-up):**
     - Oral co-trimoxazole: 6/66 treatment failures (9.1%)
     - Combined treatment: 7/68 treatment failures (10.2%)
     - Risk difference not significant: -0.01% (95% CI -0.11 to 0.09)
     - **One death during follow-up:** Injectable therapy group

2. **Straus et al (1998):**
   - **Design:** Oral co-trimoxazole (n=203) vs. oral amoxicillin (n=99)
   - **Population:** 302 children with severe pneumonia
   - **Results:**
     - Failure rates: 33% co-trimoxazole vs. 18% amoxicillin
     - RR 1.79 (95% CI 1.13-2.84)
     - **Note:** No significant antimicrobial MIC association with outcome in bacteraemic children

3. **Addo-yobo et al (2004):**
   - **Design:** Oral amoxicillin (n=857) vs. IV penicillin 2 days then oral amoxicillin (n=845)
   - **Population:** 1,702 children with severe pneumonia
   - **Results:**
     - 48-hour treatment failure: 161/845 (19%) amoxicillin vs. 167/857 (19%) penicillin
     - Risk difference not significant: -0.4% (95% CI -4.2 to 3.3)
     - Day 14 cumulative mortality: 0.2% oral vs. 1% parenteral (not statistically significant, RD -0.6%, 95% CI -0.1 to 1.3)
     - Similar recovery at days 5 and 14

4. **Atkinson et al (2007):**
   - **Design:** Eight paediatric centres in England (general district and tertiary hospitals)
   - **Population:** All but most severe pneumonia cases; 203 randomized
   - **Groups:** 7-day oral amoxicillin (n=100) vs. IV benzyl penicillin (n=103)
   - **Primary outcome:** Time for temperature <38°C for 24 continuous hours
   - **Results:** Equivalence between treatments; median 1.3 days both groups

5. **Hazir et al 2008:**
   - **Design:** Hospitalization with parenteral ampicillin 4x/day for 2 days + 3 days oral amoxicillin vs. ambulatory home treatment oral amoxicillin twice daily for 5 days
   - **Population:** 2,037 children
   - **Results (Day 6):**
     - Hospitalization group: 87/1,025 treatment failures (8.6%)
     - Ambulatory group: 77/1,012 treatment failures (7.5%)
     - Risk difference 1.1% not statistically significant (95% CI -1.3 to 3.5)
     - 14-day mortality: 5 children (0.2% total), 1 ambulatory, 4 hospitalized
     - No deaths considered associated with treatment allocation
     - Antibiotic had been changed before deaths

6. **Ashraf 2010 (Day care vs. hospital care):**
   - **Design:** Children 2-59 months with severe pneumonia
   - **Groups:** Day care (n=180) with daily injectable ceftriaxone for 5 days + feeding + supportive care vs. 24-hour hospital care (n=180)
   - **Durations:** Mean 7.1±2.3 days day care; 6.5±2.8 days hospital
   - **Primary outcome:** Treatment success (SpO₂>95% room air)
   - **Results:**
     - Day care failure: 24/180 (13.3%)
     - Hospital failure: 7/180 (3.9%)
     - RR 3.4 (95% CI 1.5-7.8)
     - Failure difference predominantly from hypoxaemia (SpO₂≤95%)
     - Follow-up readmission: 22/180 (14.1%) day care vs. 11/180 (6.4%) hospital (RR 2.0, 95% CI 0.99-4.0)
     - **3-month follow-up:** 2 hospital deaths
     - Cost per successfully treated child: US$ 114 (clinic) vs. US$ 178 (hospital)

##### 6.3.2 Benefits and Risks

**Benefits:**
- Oral antibiotic therapy favorable for health system (less expensive, reduced burden)
- Despite reported in vitro (laboratory) pneumococcus penicillin resistance, no clinical failure reports
- Oral amoxicillin more effective than oral co-trimoxazole for severe pneumonia treatment (chest indrawing)

**Risks:**
- [Further research needed on risks in high HIV prevalence settings]

#### 4.2.4 Very Severe Pneumonia

**Recommendation:**

Children aged 2-59 months with very severe pneumonia should be treated with parenteral ampicillin (or penicillin) and gentamicin as first-line treatment.

**First-line dosing:**
- **Ampicillin:** 50 mg/kg or **Benzyl penicillin:** 50,000 units/kg IM/IV every 6 hours for ≥5 days
- **PLUS Gentamicin:** 7.5 mg/kg IM/IV once daily for ≥5 days

**Second-line treatment:** Ceftriaxone for severe pneumonia failure on first-line

**Strength:** Strong recommendation  
**Evidence Quality:** Moderate quality evidence (first-line); Expert opinion (second-line)

### 4.4 Treatment of Common Causes of Fever

#### 4.4.1 Acute Bacterial Meningitis

**Recommendations:**

a) Children with acute bacterial meningitis should be treated empirically with 3rd generation cephalosporins.

**Dosing options:**
- **Ceftriaxone:** 50 mg/kg per dose IV every 12 hours OR 100 mg/kg once daily
- **OR Cefotaxime:** 50 mg/kg per dose every 6 hours
- **Duration:** 10-14 days

**Strength:** Strong recommendation  
**Evidence Quality:** Moderate quality evidence

b) Where known no significant resistance to chloramphenicol and beta-lactam antibiotics among meningitis-causing bacteria, follow national guidelines or choose either:

- **Chloramphenicol:** 25 mg/kg IM/IV every 6 hours PLUS **Ampicillin:** 50 mg/kg IM/IV every 6 hours

**OR**

- **Chloramphenicol:** 25 mg/kg IM/IV every 6 hours PLUS **Benzyl penicillin:** 60 mg/kg (100,000 units/kg) every 6 hours IM/IV

**Strength:** Conditional recommendation  
**Evidence Quality:** Moderate quality evidence

---

#### 4.4.2 Acute Otitis Media

**Recommendations:**

a) Children with acute otitis media should be treated with oral amoxicillin at 40 mg/kg twice daily for 7-10 days.

**Strength:** Strong recommendation  
**Evidence Quality:** Low quality evidence

b) Where pathogens causing acute otitis media known sensitive to co-trimoxazole, this could be used as alternative given twice daily for 7-10 days.

**Strength:** Strong recommendation  
**Evidence Quality:** Low quality evidence

---

#### 4.4.3 Chronic Suppurative Otitis Media (CSOM) - Topical Antibiotics

**Recommendations:**

a) Children with CSOM should, in addition to aural toilet by dry wicking, be treated with instillation of drops containing quinolones (ciprofloxacin, norfloxacin, ofloxacin) three times daily for two weeks.

**Strength:** Strong recommendation  
**Evidence Quality:** Low quality evidence

b) Children failing to respond to treatment should be referred for further evaluation of other CSOM causes, especially tuberculosis.

**Strength:** Strong recommendation  
**Evidence Quality:** Expert opinion

#### 4.4.4 CSOM - Topical Antiseptics and Steroids

**Recommendation:** Topical antiseptics and steroids should NOT be used for CSOM treatment in children.

**Strength:** Strong recommendation  
**Evidence Quality:** Low quality evidence

#### 4.4.5 Typhoid Fever

**Recommendations:**

a) Children with typhoid fever should be treated with fluoroquinolone as first-line treatment for 7-10 days.

**Fluoroquinolone options:** Ciprofloxacin, Gatifloxacin, Ofloxacin, Perfloxacin

**Dosing:**
- **Ciprofloxacin:** 15 mg/kg/dose orally twice daily for 7-10 days

**Strength:** Strong recommendation  
**Evidence Quality:** Moderate quality evidence

b) If treatment response poor, consider drug-resistant typhoid; treat with second-line antibiotic like 3rd generation cephalosporins or azithromycin.

**Second-line options:**
- **Ceftriaxone (IV):** 80 mg/kg per day for 5-7 days
- **OR Azithromycin:** 20 mg/kg per day for 5-7 days

**Strength:** Strong recommendation  
**Evidence Quality:** Moderate quality evidence

c) Where drug resistance to antibiotics among salmonella isolates known, follow national guidelines according to local susceptibility data.

**Strength:** Strong recommendation  
**Evidence Quality:** Moderate quality evidence

---

### 4.5 Antibiotics in Severe Acute Malnutrition (SAM)

#### 4.5.1 SAM Without Complications

**Recommendation:** In children with SAM without complications, manage according to current community case management guidelines.

**Strength:** Weak recommendation  
**Evidence Quality:** Expert opinion

#### 4.5.2 SAM With Complications

**Recommendation:** In children with SAM with complications, give parenteral antibiotics as follows:

**First-line treatment (2 days):**
- **Benzyl penicillin:** 50,000 U/kg IM/IV every 6 hours
- **OR Ampicillin:** 50 mg/kg IM/IV every 6 hours
- **THEN (5 days) oral amoxicillin:** 15 mg/kg/dose every 8 hours

**PLUS (7 days total):**
- **Gentamicin:** 7.5 mg/kg IM/IV once daily

**Strength:** Weak recommendation  
**Evidence Quality:** Low quality evidence

---

### 4.6 Oxygen Therapy and Hypoxaemia

#### 4.6.1 Pulse Oximetry for Hypoxaemia Detection

**Recommendation:** Pulse oximetry recommended to determine hypoxaemia presence and guide oxygen therapy administration in infants and children with hypoxaemia.

**Strength:** Strong recommendation  
**Evidence Quality:** Low quality evidence

---

#### 4.6.2 Clinical Signs for Hypoxaemia Detection

**Recommendations:**

a) Use pulse oximetry wherever possible for hypoxaemia detection in children with severe lower respiratory infections. If unavailable, use following clinical signs to guide oxygen therapy need:

**Primary indicators:**
- Central cyanosis
- Nasal flaring
- Inability to drink or feed (due to respiratory distress)
- Grunting with every breath
- Depressed mental state (drowsy, lethargic)

**Strength:** Strong recommendation  
**Evidence Quality:** Low quality evidence

b) In some situations and depending on overall clinical condition, children with less-specific signs may also need oxygen:

**Secondary indicators:**
- Severe lower chest wall indrawing
- Respiratory rate ≥70/min
- Head nodding

**Strength:** Strong recommendation  
**Evidence Quality:** Very low quality evidence

---

#### 4.6.3 Oxygen Therapy for Hypoxaemia Treatment

**Recommendations:**

a) Children with hypoxaemia should receive appropriate oxygen therapy.

**Strength:** Strong recommendation  
**Evidence Quality:** Low quality evidence

b) Effective oxygen delivery systems should be universal standard of care and made more widely available.

**Strength:** Strong recommendation  
**Evidence Quality:** Expert opinion

---

#### 4.6.4 Oxygen Administration Thresholds

**Recommendations:**

a) Oxygen therapy administration should be guided by pulse oximetry where available; thresholds may vary depending on altitude.

**Strength:** Strong recommendation  
**Evidence Quality:** Very low quality evidence

b) Children living at ≤2500 m above sea level should receive oxygen therapy if oxygen saturation ≤90% (measured by pulse oximetry).

**Strength:** Strong recommendation  
**Evidence Quality:** Very low quality evidence

c) In children at high altitude (>2500 m above sea level), normal oxygen saturation lower than sea level. At these altitudes, lower saturation level such as SpO₂ ≤87% could be used as oxygen threshold.

**Strength:** Recommendation  
**Evidence Quality:** Very low quality evidence

---

#### 4.6.5 Oxygen Delivery Methods

**Recommendations:**

a) Nasal prongs are preferred method for oxygen delivery in infants and children <5 years with hypoxaemia requiring oxygen therapy.

**Strength:** Strong recommendation  
**Evidence Quality:** Moderate quality evidence

b) Where nasal prongs unavailable, nasal or nasopharyngeal catheters could be used as alternative delivery methods. Face masks or head-boxes NOT recommended.

**Strength:** Strong recommendation  
**Evidence Quality:** Moderate quality evidence

---

#### 4.6.6 Criteria for Starting and Stopping Oxygen

**Recommendations:**

a) Children with hypoxaemia should be closely monitored using pulse oximetry.

**Strength:** Strong recommendation  
**Evidence Quality:** Very low quality evidence

b) Oxygen therapy should be discontinued when oxygen saturation remains stable above recommended levels for at least 15 minutes on room air in clinically stable child:

- **≤2500 m above sea level:** ≥90%
- **>2500 m above sea level:** ≥87%

**Strength:** Strong recommendation  
**Evidence Quality:** Very low quality evidence

---

### 4.7 Treatment of Hypoglycaemia

#### 4.7.1 Sublingual Sugar Administration

**Recommendation:** Sublingual sugar may be used as immediate first-aid measure managing hypoglycaemia in children in situations where intravenous glucose administration impossible or delayed.

**Strength:** Strong recommendation  
**Evidence Quality:** Low quality evidence

---

### 4.8 Choice of Intravenous Fluids

#### 4.8.1 Resuscitation Fluids

**Recommendation:** Children severely dehydrated or with shock signs should be resuscitated using isotonic IV solutions:
- Sodium chloride 0.9%
- Ringers lactate

**Strength:** Strong recommendation  
**Evidence Quality:** Low quality evidence

#### 4.8.2 Maintenance IV Fluids

**Recommendation:** For children requiring IV fluids for maintenance, options include:
- Ringers lactate solution with 5% dextrose
- Sodium chloride 0.45% with glucose 5%
- Sodium chloride 0.45% with glucose 2.5%
- 0.9% sodium chloride with glucose 5%

**Strength:** Strong recommendation  
**Evidence Quality:** Low quality evidence

#### 4.8.3 Fluids to Avoid

**Recommendation:** Low sodium-containing solutions should NOT be used due to increased hyponatraemia risk leading to cerebral oedema:
- Sodium chloride 0.18% with glucose 4%
- 5% glucose in water

**Strength:** Strong recommendation  
**Evidence Quality:** Low quality evidence

## 6. Research Gaps

The Guidelines Development Group identified critical areas requiring further primary research:

**Neonatal conditions:**
- Vitamin K prophylaxis effectiveness in varied resource settings
- Prophylactic antibiotics for infection-risk neonates
- Optimal phototherapy and exchange transfusion thresholds
- Kangaroo Mother Care in varied settings and populations
- Hypothermia prevention in very low birth weight infants

**Respiratory conditions:**
- Non-severe pneumonia and wheeze management in varied populations
- Severe and very severe pneumonia antibiotic regimens
- Long-term outcomes of oral vs. injectable antibiotic treatment

**Fever conditions:**
- Meningitis antibiotics in developing country settings
- Acute and chronic otitis media management options
- Typhoid fever treatment optimization

**Other areas:**
- Antibiotics in severe acute malnutrition
- Oxygen system effectiveness and delivery methods
- Pulse oximetry impact in resource-limited settings
